Context
Government & Corporate-Sponsored Project

A government and corporate-sponsored team of four was developing a smart library system that uses a robotic arm, AI, and a database to allow book borrowing outside of regular operating hours. Users reserve a book through a mobile app, the robotic arm retrieves it and moves it to a locker, and users can pick it up unattended — making late-night or early-morning borrowing possible. The concept was shaped around Korea's study culture, where students frequently study through the night at 24-hour study rooms (독서실) and may need to borrow textbooks in the early hours of the morning. None of the four team members had a design or front-end background, so I stepped in as a volunteer design contributor to build the app's UI from scratch.

Starting Point · 01
What the Team Had

The team's initial draft focused entirely on getting the required features down — there was no consideration of user flow, information hierarchy, or visual consistency. Screens were mapped out as a flat list of functions without grouping or structure, and the same categories appeared scattered across multiple separate pages.

Initial draft shared by the team — feature-first, without UX consideration
No user flow structure — screens were listed as independent functions with no sense of how a user would navigate between them.
Scattered categories — related content (e.g. borrowed books, reservations, wishlist) existed as separate pages with no unifying structure.
No component system — no reusable navigation, cards, or layout patterns; each screen was designed in isolation.
Unclear hierarchy — buttons, labels, and copy were inconsistent, making it hard to understand what actions were available at a glance.
Role · 02
How I Got Involved

I'm not an official member of the team — I joined as a volunteer design contributor at the request of my sibling, who is one of the four developers on the project. Since the team had no one with a design or front-end background, I was brought in to translate their feature list into a structured, usable interface.

My scope covered two things: building the UI in Figma (components and screens), and providing UX feedback as the team iterated. I worked independently — the team would share what they needed, and I'd return with designs and suggestions.

Restructuring the User Flow

Before designing any screens, I reorganized the app's structure. The original draft had too many disconnected pages — my first step was to group related content together so the navigation felt logical and minimal.

Fig.2: Restructured user flow — from login to book pick-up
Login → Home as the entry point, with notices and a quick summary of the user's current borrowing status visible immediately on the home screen.
Bottom nav with 4 tabs — Home, My Collection (내서재), Search, and More — keeping the most-used actions one tap away.
My Collection as the second tab — since the core purpose of the app is borrowing and reserving books, this was the most important section after home.
Tabbed navigation within My Collection — Borrowed Books, Wishlist, and Reservations/Locker grouped under one section instead of three separate pages.

Design · 05
Key Screens

Using the component system, I designed the full set of app screens — covering the core user journey from login to book pick-up, as well as supporting screens like search and the mobile library card.

Fig.6: Key screens — Login, Sign-up, Home, My Collection (Borrowed / Wishlist / Reservations), Search, Mobile Library Card
Home — Notice board at the top, followed by a quick My Collection summary so users can see their borrowing status without navigating away.
My Collection — Three tabs (Borrowed, Wishlist, Reservations & Locker) consolidating what was originally scattered across multiple pages.
Search — Book search with recent queries and recommended titles; results show availability status and reservation button inline.
Mobile Library Card — QR code-based digital card integrated into the user profile, eliminating the need for a physical card at the locker.
UX · 06
UX Feedback & Iterations

After the team iterated on my initial designs, I provided a round of UX feedback — focusing on clarity and reducing unnecessary friction. Most issues came from the team's developer mindset: screens were technically correct but had copy, buttons, or layout choices that would confuse a first-time user.

Copy — Replaced technical labels and placeholder text with language that reflects how users actually think about borrowing books.
Button cleanup — Removed actions that weren't relevant to the current screen state; kept only the primary action visible at each step.
Layout adjustments — Tightened spacing and visual grouping so related information reads as a unit rather than a list of independent items.

Takeaway

This was the first time I designed for a team that had no shared design language — no prior UI conventions, no visual references, and no one to review decisions with. That constraint pushed me to be more deliberate about documentation: every component needed to be self-explanatory, and every UX decision needed a rationale the developers could act on without asking follow-up questions. I also came away with a clearer sense of the gap between "technically functional" and "actually usable" — a gap that only shows up when someone without a design background builds a screen. Bridging that gap, quietly and without friction, is something I want to keep getting better at.
